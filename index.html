Show the maths

Both models are given the same starting fact: the place on Earth where the sun is directly overhead at this moment (the subsolar point). Neither is given the answer.

Globe. The sun is so distant that its rays arrive parallel, so its elevation is 90° minus the angle along the surface between you and the subsolar point: sin(el) = sin φ sin δ + cos φ cos δ cos(Δλ).

Flat. Places sit on the north-pole-centred map at distance r = (90° − φ) × 111.2 km from the pole. The sun sits at height H above the subsolar point, and the elevation is atan(H / d), where d is the map distance to that point. Its width shrinks in proportion to its distance from you.

Miss is the angle on the sky between a model's sun and the measured sun. The score is the root-mean-square miss over daytime moments only, which is the kindest test for the flat model.

Your own measurements. A stick of height h casting a shadow of length s on level ground puts the sun at atan(h / s) above the horizon, in the direction opposite the shadow. If you did not measure a bearing, only the elevations are compared.

Bending by the air. Each position is worked out as plain geometry first, then lifted by Sæmundsson's formula, 1.02′ / tan(h + 10.3 / (h + 5.11)) for a true elevation h in degrees: 29′ for something geometrically on the horizon, 5′ at 10° up, 1′ at 45°. "My conditions" multiplies that by (P / 1010 hPa) × (283 K / T). The same lift goes on the measurement and on both models.

Stars. Each star is handled exactly like the sun: both models are told the place it is directly overhead. The globe model puts it immeasurably far away. The flat model puts every star at one adjustable height, turning about the point above the north pole once a day.

Distances. On the globe the shortest path is a great circle. On the flat map it is a straight line between the two places as drawn. Along a line of longitude the two agree; east to west in the south, the flat map stretches distances to several times their real length.
